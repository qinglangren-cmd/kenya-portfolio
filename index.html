Ten days can be brief.

I came to Kenya to contribute through visual storytelling, classroom support, and community activities. I left with photographs, friendships, and questions that took much longer than ten days to process.

01 / The work

Showing up,
taking part.

My time in Homa Bay was shaped by small, practical contributions. I supported the school community through visual documentation, classroom activities, and campus events.

Visual storytelling & community support

Visual Content & Storytelling
Captured and curated photographs of daily educational activities to help document the school’s work and support its outreach and fundraising efforts.

Classroom Support
Supported local staff with classroom activities and community-based educational programs.

Campus Activities
Helped organize and facilitate student activities, including sports and group games, encouraging participation and connection.

02 / Everyday life

Learning a
different rhythm.

Homa Bay challenged some of my assumptions about what a normal day looks like. Basic routines required more thought, flexibility, and patience than I was used to.

Distance is more than a number.

The journey from Homa Bay to Nairobi covered a little over 300 kilometres, yet travelling by shuttle could take more than ten hours. Electricity and water were not always readily available; charging a camera, planning a shower, or managing a simple daily routine called for patience and adaptation.

These experiences were not always comfortable. They reminded me that convenience is often something we stop noticing when it is always within reach.

03 / People along the way

Brief encounters.
Lasting impressions.

Some of the strongest memories are attached not to a landmark, but to a conversation, a shared ride, or the generosity of someone who made an unfamiliar place feel a little less unfamiliar.

I met Fin and Cassie, who were each finding their own ways to travel and work; Nixon and his family — Scovia, Daniela, and Nigel — whose warmth made an impression on me; and Ronny, who often helped with the journeys between town and home. Later, in Mombasa, a tuk-tuk driver named Andrew added another small but memorable connection to the journey. The encounters were brief, but they remain part of how I remember Kenya.

04 / Looking back

Not every meaningful experience is an easy one.

Was the journey always enjoyable? No. There were moments of discomfort, uncertainty, and adjustment. But those moments were part of the experience, too.

Living alongside different routines and perspectives made me think more carefully about access to education, women’s opportunities, health, and the many ways people build their everyday lives. I returned with no simple conclusion — only a greater awareness of how much there is to understand beyond my own experience.
